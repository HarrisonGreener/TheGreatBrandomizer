Colour contrast for designers

Colour contrast decides whether people can actually read your design. It matters for the millions of people with low vision or colour blindness, and for everyone else reading on a phone in bright sunlight. The good news is that it's easy to measure.

What is a contrast ratio?

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) measure contrast as a ratio between the lighter and darker colour, based on how bright each one looks to the human eye. The scale runs from 1:1, which is the same colour on itself, to 21:1, which is black on white.

The numbers to remember

  • 4.5:1 is the minimum for normal body text at level AA. This is the standard most websites aim for.
  • 3:1 is the minimum for large text at level AA. Large means at least 24 pixels, or about 19 pixels if bold. The same 3:1 applies to icons, buttons and other important graphics.
  • 7:1 is the minimum for normal text at level AAA, the highest level. Large text needs 4.5:1 for AAA.

When you cash out on the Brandomizer, every pair of colours in your palette is checked and labelled AAA, AA or AA large. Pairs below 3:1 are left out of the list, because they shouldn't be used for text at all.

Why some lovely pairs fail

Contrast depends on brightness, not hue. Red and green can look very different but have almost the same brightness, which makes them hard to tell apart for many people. Pastel pink on white, or neon pink on purple, often look striking in a swatch and fail badly as text.

Building contrast into a palette

  • Always include a very dark and a very light colour. They give you at least one pairing that passes everything. Every Brandomizer palette does this automatically.
  • Use bright and pastel colours for shapes, not text. Let them fill backgrounds, illustrations and buttons, and put the text in your dark or light.
  • Check button text separately. White text on a mid-tone brand colour is one of the most common failures. Try the dark text colour instead.
  • Don't rely on colour alone. Links, errors and states should also use underlines, icons or labels, so they work for people who can't see the colour difference.

Is AAA always better?

Higher contrast isn't always nicer to read. Pure black on pure white can feel harsh on screens for long passages. Many designers use a very dark grey or brown on an off-white, which still passes AAA comfortably while feeling softer. Aim for AA everywhere and AAA for your main body text.
